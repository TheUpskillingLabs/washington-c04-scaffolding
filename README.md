## Our edge

## Why this is on OLOS

The Upskilling Labs of Greater Washington is building OLOS for itself. The region runs its own instance, the first one outside the national core, and changes it to fit how the region actually works. Whatever holds up goes back to core OLOS as a series of pull requests, so the next region starts from what Washington learned. That's the relationship: the region builds and proves, the core keeps and carries.

Every project in this pod is one of those changes, and one of those pull requests.

Three names that get mixed up:

- **OLOS** is the idea and the open-source project: an operating system for an open lab. How a community runs cycles, workshops, pods, projects and membership, in code anyone can run and change. It belongs to no single lab.
- **The Upskilling App** is the national core's live instance of OLOS at theupskillinglabs.org. It's the reference implementation, and it holds what's been built so far: a few years of one lab's needs, not the whole idea.
- **Greater Washington's instance** is the region's own copy, built from the same code, changed to fit the region. What we build here is for us first, and for OLOS second.

When you research, research The Labs, not the app. When you build, build on OLOS, not around the app's limits.


## How we research

This pod works through frame innovation (Kees Dorst, *Frame Innovation*, 2015). The problem is The Labs itself: how it teaches, how it's seen, where it lives, how it pays its way. We don't start with solutions, and we don't start from what OLOS can do today. We start with how the problem came to be, and why it's hard. Dorst's nine steps, and where they fall in this Cycle:

| Step | What it asks | When |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Archaeology | How did this come to be called a problem, by whom, in what words? What's been tried? What's assumed? | Weeks 1 and 2 |
| 2. Paradox | Why is it hard? Which things are both true and pull against each other? | Nov 7 |
| 3. Context | Who is closest to it at The Labs, and what does each of them need? | Weeks 1 and 2 |
| 4. Field | Who else, anywhere, faces something like this? What do they do? | Weeks 1 to 5 |
| 5. Themes | What shows up across all of them? | Nov 7 |
| 6. Frames | A new way of seeing the problem that makes the paradox easier to move | Week 3 |
| 7. Futures | Does the frame hold? Show it to players and see whether they lean in | Nov 14 |
| 8. Transformation | Change how The Labs works, built on OLOS, released weekly to real people | Weeks 4 to 6 |
| 9. Integration | The change becomes part of how the region runs, with someone to keep it, and goes upstream to core OLOS | Dec 8 |

Everything we learn is written into this repo.

## The research (weeks 1 and 2)

We are not solving this yet. We are finding out how it came to be called a problem, by whom, and what everyone has assumed since. We look for what we don't know, not for confirmation of what we think.

The system we're researching is the whole of The Labs: upskillers, mentors, workshop leaders, facilitators, builders, the library, the steering committee, the Board, funders, employers, the region. Not the app. Map who's in it (Dorst's *context*), then look outward to who else faces this (*field*).

Three questions:

1. **Who owns this problem, and how was it first stated?** Who said it first, when, in what words, and what did they want?
2. **What has been tried, and what happened?**
3. **What is being treated as fixed?** What's actually negotiable?

How the tension was worded, including any "How Might We", is something to dig into, not proof of the problem.

**Three ways to find out**

**Interviews.** Talk to people who have lived this. Ask about a specific time, not opinions in general: *Tell me about the last time this went wrong. What happened? What did you try? What did you assume? Who else should I talk to?* Aim for a handful per pod, from different sides of the problem.

- Upskillers who stalled or left, and ones who thrived. What did they need, and when?
- Mentors, workshop leaders and facilitators from the pilot and Cycles C01 to C03. What did they change between Cycles, and why?
- Whoever first raised tension 01 at the Oct 3 retreat. What did they see, and what did they want fixed?

**Case studies.** Find someone else who has faced something like this. Write down who they are, what they were trying to do, what they tried, what happened, and what we can't tell from outside.

- Programs that give structure and then take it away: apprenticeships, Cooperative Extension, self-directed programs like the Recurse Center, bootcamps that closed. What did they decide, and what happened?

**Records.** What we already have.

- The run of show for each past Cycle, and every change to it.
- Where "get stuck first" came from, and what happened when it was followed or broken.
- The survey responses on guidance vs. autonomy. Count them, then quote them.

Survey responses count as evidence of what people felt. Count them and quote them.

**How to log it**

1. Open an issue labeled `dig`. Start the title with `Interview:`, `Case:` or `Record:`.
2. Write: *what it is, where it came from, the date, which of the three questions it answers, and what it tells us.*
3. Say plainly when something is hearsay, and who could confirm it.

**Keep people out of the repo.** No names, no recordings, no transcripts, nothing that identifies someone. Write "a former mentor," not who. Keep your raw notes yourself.

**Nov 7:** the pod reads its finds together and writes its first paradox from the evidence.

## Contribute

You don't need to code, and you don't need to be an expert. If you're part of The Labs, or you know something about this problem, you can help. The maintainers, who are this pod's builders in residence, will get you started.

Right now, the pod is researching together. After Nov 14, each builder in residence leads a project and recruits a team. You can join the research now and a project later, or both.

1. Introduce yourself on the pinned issue: what you know, and what you'd like to look into.
2. A maintainer adds you to the repo and points you to one thing to find out: an interview, a case study, or a record.
3. Log what you find as a `dig` issue, as above.
4. Changes to this README go through a pull request, and a maintainer reviews them.

New to GitHub? Say so on the pinned issue and a maintainer will walk you through it.

## Projects

Projects come out of the research. Each builder in residence leads one, chooses the frame it tests, and recruits a team of contributors. Projects are built on Greater Washington's instance of OLOS, the Labs' open-source platform, so the code lives in the region's OLOS fork on a branch named after this repo plus the project, `washington-c04-scaffolding-projectname`, with no hyphens inside the project name (for example, `washington-c04-scaffolding-mentorbench`). This repo carries a branch of the same name holding the project's README: the frame it tests, the team, what happened. `main` holds the pod's shared research.

By Dec 8, each project is a working app in real use, with a written account of what it tested and what happened.
