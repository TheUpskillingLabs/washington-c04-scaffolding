# Scaffolding

**How much structure does a learner need, when, and from whom?**

## What this is

This is one of four Research Pods in **Build Cycle C04**, run by The Upskilling Labs of Greater Washington. A Build Cycle is six weeks in which a group of neighbors picks a hard problem, researches it properly, and builds something real in response. This Cycle, for the first time, the problem is The Labs itself. This pod took one piece of it: the question above.

We use **frame creation**, a research method from designer Kees Dorst (*Frame Innovation*, 2015). The idea is simple. Hard problems stay hard because everyone is looking at them the same way. If you study the problem carefully, you find a **paradox**: two things that are both true and pull against each other. Then you look for a **frame**: a new way of seeing the problem that makes the paradox easier to move. Only then do you build.

By **Dec 8**, each builder in residence in this pod ships one working change to how The Labs runs, built on OLOS, used by real people, and written up.

## Where we started

On sensemaking day (Oct 24) we worked from intuition. We went looking for the paradox and came away with hunches. Here's how the pod first put it:

From the October 3 retreat, tension 01: **guidance vs. autonomy.** It drew more survey responses than any other.

As the pod first wrote it:

- Structure, framework and syllabus **vs.** free-form "learn by doing" with guides
- Peer-to-peer support and discovery **vs.** top-down instruction

**Who seems to be involved:** Upskillers · mentors · workshop leaders · facilitators

We own what happens in the room. How progress is seen from outside is [Wayfinding](https://github.com/TheUpskillingLabs/washington-c04-wayfinding). We read each other's repos every week.

## Why we build on OLOS

OLOS is The Labs' open-source platform: the software a lab runs on. The Upskilling App at theupskillinglabs.org is the national organization's copy of it, less than a year old. Greater Washington is setting up its own copy for this Cycle. We change ours to fit how the region works, and anything that holds up goes back to the main OLOS project as a pull request, so the next region starts from what we learned. Research The Labs, not the app. Build on OLOS, not around the app's limits.

## The nine steps, and when

Dorst's steps in his words (Dorst & Kokotovich, 2015), and what this pod does for each.

| Step | Dorst | Here | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Archaeology | "Analyzing the history of the problem owner and of the initial problem formulation." | Who first called this a problem, in what words. What's been tried, anywhere. What everyone assumes. | Now to Nov 7 |
| 2. Paradox | "Analyzing the problem situation: what makes this hard?" | Written from what archaeology found, not from Saturday's hunches. | Nov 7 |
| 3. Context | "Analyzing the inner circle of stakeholders." | The people closest to this at The Labs, and what each needs. | Now to Nov 7 |
| 4. Field | "Exploring the broader societal field." | Who else, anywhere, faces something like this, and what they do. | Now to Nov 28 |
| 5. Themes | "Investigating the themes that emerge in the broader field." | What shows up across everyone we talked to and read. | Nov 7 |
| 6. Frames | "Identifying patterns in the themes to create frames." | Each builder in residence proposes one frame and a rough prototype. | Nov 8 to 14 |
| 7. Futures | "Exploring the possible outcomes and value propositions for the various stakeholders." | Frames shown to real people. Builders pitch; contributors choose a project. | Nov 14 |
| 8. Transformation | "Investigate the change in stakeholders' strategies and practices required for implementation." | Build the change on the region's OLOS. Release weekly to real people. Change it from what they do. | Nov 15 to Dec 5 |
| 9. Integration | "Draw lessons from the new approach and identify new opportunities within the network." | In use, someone keeps it, written up, taught back, pull request to OLOS. | Dec 8 |

Budget **4 to 6 hours of your own time a week**, not counting what your coding agent does. If a week needs more, cut scope.

## What we're collecting now (archaeology, due Nov 7)

Archaeology means finding out how this became a problem and what has already been tried. **Not just by us.** Anyone, anywhere, who has faced this problem counts. Three kinds of evidence:

| Kind | What it is | What it must include |
|---|---|---|
| **Interview** | A conversation with one person who has lived this problem, here or elsewhere. Ask about a specific time: *Tell me about the last time this went wrong. What happened? What did you try? What did you assume? Who else should I talk to?* | Their role (not their name), when you spoke, what they said in their words, what it tells us |
| **Case study** | One organization or program, anywhere, that tried to solve something like this | Who they are, what they tried, what happened, how we know, what we can't tell from outside |
| **Record** | Something that already exists: a document, a dataset, a past decision, a survey count | What it is, where it lives, when it's from, what it says |

**Where to look first**

- **Other programs** that teach by doing: apprenticeships, Cooperative Extension, coding bootcamps (including ones that closed), self-directed schools. How much structure did they give, when did they take it away, and what happened?
- **Our own past Cycles.** What structure the pilot and C01 to C03 each used, what changed between them, and why. Where the rule "get stuck first" came from.
- **How this became a problem here.** Who raised "guidance vs. autonomy" at the Oct 3 retreat, in what words, and what they wanted fixed.
- **The survey.** Every response about guidance vs. autonomy. Count them. Quote them.

**How to turn it in**

One GitHub issue per piece of evidence. Title it `Interview:`, `Case:` or `Record:` and a few words. In the body, answer these in order:

1. **What it is.** One or two sentences.
2. **Where it came from.** The role of the person, the name of the organization, or the location of the document.
3. **When.** The date of the conversation, or the date of the thing.
4. **What it says.** Facts and quotes. Keep your opinion separate.
5. **What it tells us about the problem.** Your read, labeled as your read.
6. **Is any of this hearsay?** If so, who could confirm it.

**No names, recordings or transcripts in this repo.** Write "a former mentor," not who. Keep raw notes on your own computer. This repo is public.

## Projects

After Nov 14, each builder in residence leads one project and recruits a team. A project is a change to how The Labs works that a real person uses, built on the region's OLOS and sent to the main project as a pull request. Code lives in the region's OLOS fork on a branch named `washington-c04-scaffolding-projectname`, no hyphens inside the project name (for example, `washington-c04-scaffolding-mentorbench`). This repo gets a branch of the same name holding the project's README. `main` holds the pod's research.
